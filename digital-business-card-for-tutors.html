How a tutor actually gets students

Almost no tutor is found by a stranger typing a subject into a search box. A parent mentions at pickup that their son's maths has turned around, and the parent beside them asks who. A class rep pastes a name into the year-group chat the week mock results come home. A teacher who may not tutor their own pupils names someone who can. Each time, your details travel through somebody else to a parent who has never met you.

So the card is opened cold, on a phone, in a busy group chat. It has to introduce you without you in the room: which subjects and levels, whether you teach online or come to the house, what check you hold, and how to book a first lesson. If the parent has to ask any of that in the chat, the recommendation has lost its momentum.

Whether the card arrives as a link in the chat, a QR code on a library flyer or the signature of your reply to an enquiry, it opens the same live card, so the availability a parent sees is this term's, not the slots you had when the flyer went up.

What goes on a tutor's card

  • Subjects, levels and exam boards. GCSE maths, A-level chemistry on the AQA specification, SAT prep, Year 6 reading. Parents look for the exact exam their child is sitting.
  • Online, in person, or both. If you travel, the areas you cover; if online, the platform you teach on. It stops enquiries from parents on the wrong side of town.
  • Your background or safeguarding check. The type and the year, worded as your certificate words it. It is the line a parent looks for first.
  • Qualifications and classroom experience. A teaching qualification, your degree, years marking for an exam board or teaching the course in a school.
  • This term's availability. Which evenings and weekend slots are open, or that you run a waiting list. Change it the day a slot fills.
  • Rates and how lessons run. Hourly rate, session length, cancellation notice. Parents compare on these before they message you.
  • A way to book a trial lesson. A booking link, plus WhatsApp and a phone number for parents who would rather message than call.

What a parent needs to see before booking a tutor's trial lesson

The parent opening your card is deciding whether to let a stranger spend an hour a week with their child, often in their own home. Before they message you they settle four things: that you teach the exact course, that you can fit their week, that you are safe, and what it costs. A card that answers those on the first screen gets the parent's message.

Put the check and the qualification near the top, and make the booking route the obvious next tap. One line saying you taught the course in a school or marked it for the exam board does more than a paragraph on your teaching philosophy.

The parent or teacher passing you on matters too. A parent answering the year-group chat wants a link they can paste without explaining it, and one that still works when somebody asks again in January. A class teacher or a tutoring agency with a list hands over your card, not a description of you, so it has to be right whenever it is opened.

What changes in a tutor's year and has to stay current

A tutor's year runs on the school calendar. September and January fill your evenings; the weeks before exams bring parents who want three sessions a week; the summer empties out or turns into bridging work. Your rate moves with the academic year. An exam board changes its specification and the course you list needs a new name.

Each of those details is already written somewhere you cannot reach: the flyer on the library board, the card in the music shop window, the message a parent forwarded in April. Nobody re-forwards a chat message to say Tuesdays are now full. With a digital card, the flyer's QR code and every forwarded link open one live card: edit the availability line once and April's message is right again.

Safeguarding checks, teacher titles and claims on a tutor's card

Tutoring is lightly regulated in most places, but three things on your card do fall under somebody's rules, and those differ by country, state and school, so check each for where you work. The first is the safeguarding check. Which check a self-employed tutor can hold and what you may call it in marketing vary by region; state the type and date exactly as your certificate does.

The second is your teaching title. Words such as qualified teacher or specialist can be protected or defined terms in some jurisdictions, and a school's policy may bar you from naming the school or tutoring its own pupils. The third is exam results. Grade guarantees and pass-rate claims you cannot document are the kind of thing advertising rules tend to cover, so describe what past students achieved rather than promising what a new one will.

Setting up a tutor's card

  1. Open the builder and enter your name with the qualification letters you are entitled to use, your subjects and levels as the job title (Maths and physics tutor, GCSE and A-level), and your tutoring name as the company.
  2. Add a phone number and WhatsApp for parents who message rather than call, an email, the areas you travel to as the address, and your booking page as the Book a call link.
  3. Use the tagline for your check and this term's availability (Enhanced DBS 2026, Tuesday and Thursday evenings open), and a custom link or text line for your rates and exam boards.
  4. Add a profile photo so parents recognise you at the door, pick a theme colour, then share by QR code on flyers, by link into the group chats that recommend you, and in your email signature.

Frequently asked questions from tutors

What should a tutor's digital business card include?

The subjects, levels and exam boards you teach, whether lessons are online or in person and the area you cover, your qualifications, the safeguarding check you hold and its year, this term's open slots, your rates, and a link to book a trial lesson. Word the check exactly as your certificate does.

Can I change my availability and rates after parents have saved the card?

Yes. The link and QR code open the live card, not a copy. When September fills your Tuesdays, or you raise rates for the new academic year, edit the card once and every parent who saved it last term sees the current version.

What should a tutor say about background checks on the card?

State the type and date of the check as they appear on your certificate. Which checks a self-employed tutor can hold, and what you may call them in marketing, differ by country and state, so check your region's rules before you publish and update the card when a renewal comes through.

Can a school teacher who tutors privately use a card like this?

Many do, but schools and education authorities often restrict tutoring your own pupils or naming the school in your marketing. Check your contract and your school's policy first, then describe yourself on the card in the terms they allow.
