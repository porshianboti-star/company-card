How coaches and therapists actually get clients

Few coaches are hired from a search result. A past client mentions your name to a colleague who is stuck, someone hears you on a podcast, or an HR manager asked to find an executive coach for a new director asks around. Each time your card is passed on by a third party, so it has to work without you in the room: say in one line who you coach and on what, and make the discovery call the obvious next step.

For a therapist or counsellor the route is more careful: a GP, a school counsellor, or a coach who has recognised that a client’s problem is clinical passes on your details. Before that person emails a therapist they want three things settled: whether you work with their kind of issue, whether you see clients online or in a room and where, and whether you have space. A card carrying your specialism, session format and current availability answers all three.

What a prospective coaching or therapy client checks before booking

  • A niche line, not a coaching title. “Leadership coaching for first-time managers” or “Counselling for anxiety and burnout in healthcare workers” tells a referrer whether to pass you on; “Certified Coach” does not.
  • Your discovery-call or booking link. Many coaching engagements begin with an introductory call, so let a prospective client pick a slot from the card instead of emailing to ask for one.
  • Session format and location. Online, in person or both, and the city if sessions happen in a room.
  • Credentials and the body behind them. Credential letters, your registering body and, for therapists, a licence or registration number if your board expects it on marketing material.
  • Availability for new coaching or therapy clients. “Taking new coaching clients from October” or “Therapy waiting list open” saves a polite dead-end exchange and is one line to edit when a client finishes.
  • A practice contact route, not your personal mobile. A work email and a business number, so a client in distress at 11pm reaches your practice voicemail.

Who passes a coach’s card on, and what they need from it

Referral partners are the quiet engine of a coaching or therapy practice: coaches send clients to therapists when the work turns clinical, therapists send clients to coaches when it turns to careers, physiotherapists cross-refer with health coaches. Each of those practitioners needs your niche and availability in seconds, and to trust that the coach or therapist they are recommending still has space. Share your card link with every practitioner you refer to, so a referral is a tap rather than a search through old emails.

Corporate coaching has a different reader: the HR or learning manager compiling a shortlist of executive coaches, who needs your practice name, credential and niche in a form they can forward to the sponsor and the coachee. A link the HR manager can drop into a supplier form travels better than a photo of a paper card.

What changes in a coaching or therapy practice and has to stay current

Your one-to-one slots fill and you open a waiting list, then a client finishes and you have space again. You launch a group programme with a cohort start date, move from a rented therapy room to online-only, complete a new credential, or switch scheduling tools so the discovery-call link you gave out at a workshop stops working.

Each of those details is sitting somewhere you cannot easily fix: the closing slide attendees photographed at your talk, show notes a podcast host published a year ago, the leaflet you left at a GP surgery, your email signature under every session confirmation. Because the QR code and link open the live card, the referrer who saved your details six months ago still reaches the current booking link and today’s availability line.

Board and professional-body rules on titles, licences and coaching claims

Therapy and counselling are regulated in many places and coaching mostly is not, which is why the wording on a coach’s or therapist’s card matters. Titles such as psychologist, psychotherapist or licensed counsellor are protected in many jurisdictions, so check your state or professional body’s rule before you use one. Some boards also expect a licence or registration number on marketing material, and a card carrying your name and therapy services should be treated as marketing.

Coaches have the opposite problem: with no licensing body, the credential letters and the body that issued them do the reassuring, so name both rather than writing “certified”, and keep outcome promises off a coach’s card, since accrediting bodies’ codes generally require accurate claims about what coaching delivers. If a supervisor points out a title you should not be using, correct the card once and every QR code on your slides and intake forms follows.

Setting up a coach’s or therapist’s card

  1. Open the builder and enter your name with the credential letters you are entitled to use, your coaching or therapy niche as a one-line headline, and your practice name.
  2. Add your discovery-call or booking link, a practice email and a business number, leaving your personal mobile off, and state whether you see clients online, in person or both, and where.
  3. Add your registering body and, if your board expects it on marketing material, your licence or registration number, then your practice logo and brand colour.
  4. Share it by QR code, link or email signature: the QR code on your closing workshop slide and intake forms, the link in podcast show notes and with every practitioner you refer to. Edit the card the day a cohort opens or your therapy waiting list closes.

Questions coaches and therapists ask

What should a coach's digital business card include?

A one-line statement of who you coach and on what, your discovery-call or booking link, session format and city, your credential letters and the body that issued them, your current availability, and a practice email and business number. Lead with the niche; a referrer passes a coach's card on from that line alone.

Do therapists and counsellors need to show a licence number on a digital business card?

That depends on the board you are registered with and where you practise. Some state boards expect a licence or registration number and the exact protected title on marketing material, and a card carrying your therapy services should be treated as marketing, so check your board's rule and add both in the form it specifies.

Can a prospective client book a discovery call straight from the card?

Yes, add the link from whichever scheduling tool you use and the person a past client just referred can choose a discovery-call slot from the card without emailing to arrange one. If you change scheduling tools, update the discovery-call link once and every QR code on your slides and show notes opens the new one.

Can I keep my personal mobile number off a therapist's card?

Yes, put only the routes you want therapy clients to use: a practice email, a business number and your booking link. Clients in distress will call whatever number they have, so the card is where you set that boundary.

Can one card cover one-to-one coaching and a group programme?

Yes, and it saves a referrer choosing between two cards. Keep the niche line about the one-to-one work, add the group programme as a second link labelled with its cohort start date, and remove it when the cohort is full; the QR code on your workshop slide never changes.
