How a stylist or barber actually fills the chair

Almost nobody finds a new hairdresser through a directory. A colleague turns up on Monday with fresh colour and gets asked who did it. A father brings his son to the barber he has used for years. A bride books a trial, and a week later four bridesmaids need your number. Each time your name is passed along by a client, usually as a screenshot, weeks before the new person books.

That hand-off is what the card has to survive. Whoever receives it needs to see your work, what you charge for the service they want, which salon you are working from this month, and a booking route that works at eleven at night with nothing to install.

The client already in your chair has a simpler need: to find you again in six weeks. A QR code in the corner of the mirror, on the aftercare card that goes home with a colour client, or in the appointment confirmation text all open the same live card, so the link they save today still works after you change booking systems or move chairs.

What goes on a stylist's or barber's card

  • Your name, then the salon you work from. Clients follow the stylist, not the shop, so put the salon on the company line where it can change when you move chairs.
  • Your booking link as the main action. Whichever system you book through, the client should be able to rebook from the chair before they pay.
  • Instagram or a gallery of your work. A colourist is chosen on before-and-after photos, a barber on fades and beard work.
  • Services and a price guide. Cuts from, colour by consultation, beard trim, extensions, bridal. It saves the message asking what balayage costs on long hair.
  • Hours and how you take new clients. Walk-ins welcome, appointment only, or books closed with a waitlist. Change it the week it changes.
  • Your salon policies in one line. Deposit for colour appointments, cancellation notice, patch test 48 hours before a first colour service.
  • Your licence number, if your board expects it. Many boards require the licence at your station and some want it on advertising too. Check your state board's rule.

Chair renters and mobile stylists: the client follows you, not the shop

If you rent a chair or a suite, the salon's card is not yours. The client who found you there needs to reach you at the next salon, and the old front desk has no reason to forward them. Your own card carries your booking link, your number and the current shop address, so when you move across town the link a client saved last spring still lands on your chair.

Mobile hairdressers and barbers who visit care homes, offices and wedding venues have no mirror to stick a QR code on. Put your service area and travel charge on the card, add the link to the message confirming a home visit, and keep it in your email signature when you quote a wedding party. The mother of the bride who forwards that email to her sister is your next booking.

What changes at a salon and has to stay current

Prices go up when your colour and product costs do, and colour prices move more often than cuts. Salon hours shift around school holidays and the December rush. The salon changes its booking software and the old link dies. You close your books to new clients, or open them again when a colleague leaves.

Each of those details is printed somewhere: the card at the mirror, the aftercare leaflet, the appointment card in the client's purse, the window decal, the flyer from the wedding fair. Nobody reprints all of that for one price rise. With a digital card every one of those QR codes opens the same live card: change the price line once and the appointment card printed last year is right again.

Cosmetology and barber board rules on licences and advertising

Hairdressing, barbering, nails and lashes are licensed trades in many places, and a card that lists your prices and services is advertising. The rules differ by country and state, so check each point with your state board of cosmetology or barbering, or the council that registers salons where you are. Some boards require your personal licence number on advertising as well as at the station, some also want the salon's establishment licence, and peels, laser and injectables often need a different licence altogether.

If you call yourself a master barber or a colour specialist, check what your board allows before you publish. If the guidance changes, or an inspector points at a line on your card, correct it once and every printed QR code follows.

Setting up a salon or barber card

  1. Open the builder and enter your name, your role (stylist, colourist, barber, nail tech) as the job title, and the salon you work from as the company, or your own trading name if you rent a chair.
  2. Add the number clients text to book, your booking page as the booking link, and your Instagram or gallery so a new client can see your work first.
  3. Put your price guide, hours and new-client status in the tagline, add your deposit and patch-test policy, and your licence number if your board expects it on advertising.
  4. Add a photo of you and your brand colour, then share by QR code at the mirror and on aftercare cards, by link in booking confirmations, and in your email signature when you quote weddings.

Frequently asked questions from stylists and barbers

What should a hairstylist's or barber's digital business card include?

Your name and the salon you work from, a booking link, your Instagram or a gallery of your work, a price guide, hours, whether you take new clients or walk-ins, and your deposit, cancellation and patch-test policy. Add your licence number if your state board requires it on advertising.

How do I get a client to rebook from the chair with a digital card?

Put the QR code in the corner of the mirror or on the aftercare card that goes home with them. The client scans it while the cut is fresh, books the next visit from your booking link before they pay, and keeps the card in their phone for next time.

I rent a chair and might move salons or change my prices. Does the card still work?

Yes. The card belongs to you, not the salon, and the QR code opens the live card rather than a copy of it. Change the shop name and address when you move, raise your colour prices or close your books to new clients, and every mirror card and aftercare leaflet you handed out at the old place shows the new details. Your booking link and number stay the same, so clients follow you.

Do I need to put my cosmetology or barber licence number on the card?

It depends on your state board or licensing council. Many boards require the licence at your station and some also want the number on advertising, which a card listing prices may count as. Check your board's rule; if it asks for the number, add it once and every printed QR code follows.
