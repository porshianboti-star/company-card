How a freelancer actually gets the next job

Very little freelance work comes from a stranger searching for your discipline. A client from last spring is asked who built their website and forwards your name in a group chat. An agency producer loses a retoucher before a shoot and rings the freelancers already in their phone. Someone in a marketers' community asks for a copywriter and a past client pastes your link.

Each time your details are passed on by someone who is not the buyer, days or weeks before the buyer acts, and who remembers that you do websites and little else. Whoever opens the card needs to see what you do and for whom, work that proves it, whether you can start this month, and a way to reach you that needs nothing installed.

Paper cannot carry your next opening, and last year's PDF one-pager shows last year's niche. A digital card holds all four, and because the QR code, link and email signature open the live version, the client who forwards you in six months forwards what you do then.

What a client needs to see on a freelancer's card

  • Your discipline and who it is for. Brand and web designer for small food brands tells a referred prospect in one line whether you fit. Freelance designer leaves them guessing.
  • Your trading name next to your own. A client searches their inbox for whichever name was on the invoice, so carry both.
  • A link to finished work. Portfolio, case study, showreel or published piece. A prospect opens it before replying, so lead with your best recent project.
  • When you are next free. Booking projects from November, in the tagline. It is the first thing a referred client asks and the detail that changes most.
  • A booking route. A discovery-call link or enquiry email, so a ready prospect takes a slot instead of meaning to write later.
  • Where you work from and how. City, time zone, remote or on-site. A producer booking a shoot day, or a client eight hours ahead, wants this settled first.

What an agency producer or past client needs before they refer you

Two people who are not your client bring much of a freelancer's work: the studio that books overflow and the past client who recommends. Both pick whoever is already in their phone and easiest to pass on.

For the producer, the card should say what you take on (motion graphics, retouching, long-form copy, e-commerce builds), whether you work on their premises or remotely, and how you price; day rate on request is enough. For the past client, it should be forwardable: a link pasted into a message, not a paper card to photograph or a PDF buried in an old thread.

Send the link with every final invoice and keep it in your email signature, so it sits in every thread they might search later.

What changes in a freelance business and has to stay current

Your availability moves every month. Rates go up in January. You narrow your niche, or add a service clients kept asking for. You register a trading name, later a company, and the name on the invoice changes. The portfolio moves host and the old address dies.

Every one of those is printed or pasted somewhere: proposal footers, the invoice template, the email signature, the sticker on the laptop lid, the table card at a market stall, your community profiles. Nobody re-edits two years of proposals for one rate change, so most of those copies are wrong. With a digital card the QR code and link point to one live card: change the availability line once and the proposal you sent last spring is right again.

Trading names, protected titles and NDAs on a freelancer's card

Freelancing is lightly regulated next to medicine or law, but three things catch people out. First, business names. Trade under a name that is not your own and your company registrar or tax office may expect your legal name, registration number or address on business correspondence, which can include a card or website. Check what yours require; if you incorporate later, edit the card once and every old invoice's QR code shows the new entity.

Second, titles. Where your field has a professional body (translators, bookkeepers, architects, engineers, therapists), words such as certified, chartered, sworn or registered may be reserved for members or licence holders. Check your body's rule before the word goes on the card.

Third, client work. The logos and case studies you show may be covered by a confidentiality clause in the contract you signed. Check it before naming a client on the card; if it is silent, ask.

Setting up a freelancer's card

  1. Open the builder and enter your name, your discipline and who you serve as the job title (Copywriter for B2B software), and your trading name as the company.
  2. Add an email on your own domain, a phone or WhatsApp number, your portfolio as the website and a discovery-call link as the booking link. The free card holds five links, so choose: work, booking, email, one messaging route, one social that shows finished projects.
  3. Put your next opening, base and time zone in the tagline (Booking from November, Leeds, remote), and use a custom link for a showreel, published piece or rate card if you share one.
  4. Add a profile photo and a theme colour that matches your portfolio, then share by QR code on proposals, invoices and the laptop sticker, by link at project hand-over, and in your email signature.

Frequently asked questions from freelancers

Is this a freelancer's business card or a business credit card?

A business card: the contact card you send a client or an agency producer, not a credit card or financing product. Searches for self-employed business card return both; CompanyCard replaces the paper card in your wallet, not the payment card.

What should a freelancer's digital business card include?

Your discipline and who it is for, your trading name next to your own, a link to finished work, when you are next free, and a discovery-call link or enquiry email. Check your professional body's rule before adding a title such as certified or chartered.

Can I change my rates, niche or availability after the QR code is printed on invoices and stickers?

Yes. The QR code and link point to the card, not to a snapshot of it. Change the positioning line, mark yourself booked until a given month or swap the portfolio link, and every proposal, invoice, sticker and closing slide already out there shows the current version. Edits are unlimited.

Can a sole trader put a trading name on the card instead of their own name?

Put the trading name as the company and your own name as the name, so a client searching an inbox for either finds you. Some registrars and tax offices expect the legal name or a registration number on business correspondence, so check their rules. If you incorporate later, edit the card once and every printed QR code shows the new name.
