Where a small business actually picks up its next customer

A two-chair barbershop, a one-van plumber and a bakery with a Saturday stall get their work the same way: somebody who already bought from you tells somebody who has not, at the school gate, in the street WhatsApp group, at the till while you box a cake. The person who eventually rings you was rarely there when your name came up.

So your details travel before they earn anything. A customer photographs the sticker on your van for her sister. The stallholder on the next pitch passes your card to a caterer asking about sourdough. Whatever arrives has to open on a phone with nothing to install and say what you sell, when you are open and how to get a price.

Which means the card has to be shareable wherever the recommendation happens: a QR code on the van and the shop door, a link in the group chat, the signature on every quote. All three open the same live card, so the customer who calls six weeks later sees this week's hours and number.

What a customer needs to find on a small business owner's card

  • You and the business, both named. A regular asks for “Dana at the bakery”; the caterer's bookkeeper wants “Maple & Rye Ltd” on the invoice.
  • What you sell, in the words people ask for it. “Boiler repairs, bathroom fitting, landlord safety certificates” is something a tenant can repeat to a landlord; “plumbing and heating solutions” is not.
  • Where you are, or where you go. A shop's street address, one tap from the map app; for a mobile trade, the towns you cover, because a stranger's first question is whether you come out to them.
  • When you are open. Hours, market days, the last order date for celebration cakes, for whoever opens the card at nine at night.
  • One tap to order, book or ask for a quote. A pre-order page, a booking calendar or a number that takes texts and WhatsApp; most small jobs go to whoever replies first.
  • The numbers your trade puts on paperwork. Registered company name, tax registration number where your country expects one on advertising, food hygiene registration, or the licence number for gas, electrical or pest control work.

What a wholesale or trade buyer wants from a small business card

Your biggest customers are often other businesses: the café that takes forty loaves before seven, the letting agent whose flats always need a locksmith, the builder who subcontracts every kitchen's tiling. They rarely buy on the day they meet you; they save your details and call the week their usual supplier lets them down.

Write the card for that week: what you can supply and in what quantity, how far ahead to order, whether you invoice on thirty days or take payment on delivery, and that you carry public liability insurance for jobs where their client will ask. A trade email or direct number says they will not sit behind the Saturday queue.

What changes at a small business between one print run and the next

Winter hours, summer hours, the fortnight you close in August. The Sunday market you drop once the shop opens. A second van with a second driver and phone. A booking app swapped for a cheaper one, a landline finally cancelled. Your first employee, bigger premises, a new trading name after the partnership splits.

Each of those is printed on something you paid for: the van livery, the window vinyl, the stall banner, the flyers that went through every letterbox, the footer on every invoice. Nobody rewraps a van for a new phone number. When the QR code on all of those opens one live card, you change the hours once and last spring's flyer stops sending people to a closed door.

Trading names, licence numbers and prices on a small business card

The card is advertising, and the rules differ by country, state and trade, so ask your company registrar, your trade's licensing body or the local small business office about each point. A limited company is commonly expected to show its registered name, sometimes its registration number, on letters and web pages; a sole trader using a business name may also need to show their own name and a contact address.

In a licensed trade the licence number may need to appear on anything advertising the work: gas, electrical and pest control are the usual examples. Food businesses may need to display a hygiene rating, and prices shown to retail customers may need to include sales tax or VAT. If a letter says your card is missing something, fix it once and the van, the window and the flyers follow.

Setting up a small business card

  1. Open the builder and put your own name in Full name, Owner in Job title and your trading name in Company; add the registered name or registration number as a Custom link shown as text where your country expects it.
  2. Add the Phone that takes texts, WhatsApp if customers message you there, the shop's Address so it opens in maps, and your pre-order, quote or booking page as the Book a call link; a mobile trade puts the towns it covers in the Tagline instead.
  3. Use the Tagline for what you sell and when you trade (“Sourdough, celebration cakes, wholesale. Tue–Sun 7–3”), and Custom links for how customers pay, your reviews page and a trade Email if other businesses buy from you.
  4. Upload your logo, pick your brand colour and turn on the QR code, then put the QR code on the van, the window and your invoices, drop the link in the group chats where customers recommend you, and add it to your email signature.

Yes. There is no seat minimum. Invite each new person from the Team page with a single-use invite link that is valid for 14 days; on the Business plan each person added is $10 a month billed yearly.

What should a small business owner put on a digital business card?

Your own name and the trading name, what you sell in the words customers ask for it, the shop address or the area your van covers, hours or market days, a phone that takes texts and WhatsApp, a link to order, book or get a quote, how you take payment, a trade contact if other businesses buy from you, and any registered name or licence number your trade expects on advertising.

Yes. Both the QR code and the link open your live card, not a copy made on the day you printed. Change your winter hours or swap your ordering page, and the van livery, window vinyl, flyers and invoice footers you paid for last year all open the new version.

What happens to my card when I hire my first employee?

They get their own card with the same trading name, logo and business details plus their own name, role and number, so a customer who dealt with your apprentice can still find the business. Team plans are priced per person with no seat minimum, so a two-person business pays for two.

Can a small business use the card instead of a website?
